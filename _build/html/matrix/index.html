💡️ Vectors, Matrices and Linear Algebra

School of Oceanography, University of Washington

Models that involve several interacting populations or sub-populations can involve lots of terms, and manipulating the resulting equations can get pretty messy. To make handling such systems of equations more convenient, mathematicians have invented vector and matrix notation. Vectors and matrices allow us to write these equations in a concise way, so we can see the forest for the trees.

Basic Matrix Facts¶

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers, such as

M=(m1,1m1,2…m1,Nm2,1m2,2…m2,Nm3,1m3,2…m3,N⋮⋮⋮mN,1mN,2…mN,N)\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix} m_{1,1} & m_{1,2} & \dots & m_{1,N} \\ m_{2,1} & m_{2,2} & \dots & m_{2,N} \\ m_{3,1} & m_{3,2} & \dots & m_{3,N} \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ m_{N,1} & m_{N,2} & \dots & m_{N,N} \end{pmatrix}M=⎝⎛​m1,1​m2,1​m3,1​⋮mN,1​​m1,2​m2,2​m3,2​⋮mN,2​​…………​m1,N​m2,N​m3,N​⋮mN,N​​⎠⎞​
(1)

The example in (1) is a square N×NN \times NN×N matrix, but in general there can be any nunber of rows and columns.

  • The elements of a matrix are indicated by subscripts: in mi,jm_{i,j}mi,j​,

    • the first subscript, iii, specifies the row

    • the second subscript, jjj, specifies the column

A vector is either a row or a column of numbers, i.e., a N×1N \times 1N×1 or 1×N1 \times N1×N matrix, e.g.,

P=(p1p2p3⋮pN),\mathbf{P} = \begin{pmatrix} p_1 \\ p_2 \\ p_3 \\ \vdots \\ p_N \end{pmatrix},P=⎝⎛​p1​p2​p3​⋮pN​​⎠⎞​,
(2)

  • Vectors and matrices are often denoted in a way that distinguishes them from scalar variables. Common choices include:

    • boldface, such as P\mathbf{P}P or M\mathbf{M}M

    • overbars, such as Pˉ\bar{P}Pˉ for a vector and Mˉˉ\bar{\bar{M}}Mˉˉ for a matrix

    Different conventions are used by different authors; a well-written presentation will indicate what is meant by each type of notation.

  • The diagonal of a matrix is all the entries for which the row number equals the column number.

  • The transpose of a matrix is obtained by flipping along the diagonal, so that rows become columns and columns become rows. There are several frequently used notations for a transpose, including P′\mathbf{P}'P′, PT\mathbf{P}^TPT and Pt\mathbf{P}^tPt. For example, the transpose of (2) is

    PT=(p1p2p3…pN).\mathbf{P}^T = \begin{pmatrix} p_1 & p_2 & p_3 & \dots & p_N \end{pmatrix}.PT=(p1​​p2​​p3​​…​pN​​).
    (3)

Matrix math¶

Addition and subtraction¶

Matrix addition and subtraction are defined only if the two matrices are the same size, in which case corresponding entries are added or subtracted.

(a1a2a3⋮aN)+(b1b2b3⋮bN)=(a1+b1a2+b2a3+b3⋮aN+bN)\begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \\ a_3 \\ \vdots \\ a_N \end{pmatrix} +\begin{pmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \\ \vdots \\ b_N \end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix} a_1+b_1 \\ a_2+b_2 \\ a_3+b_3 \\ \vdots \\ a_N+b_N \end{pmatrix}⎝⎛​a1​a2​a3​⋮aN​​⎠⎞​+⎝⎛​b1​b2​b3​⋮bN​​⎠⎞​=⎝⎛​a1​+b1​a2​+b2​a3​+b3​⋮aN​+bN​​⎠⎞​
(4)

Matrix addition and subtraction are distributive:

A+B=B+AA−B=−(B−A)\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A} \\ \mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B} = -(\mathbf{B} - \mathbf{A})A+B=B+AA−B=−(B−A)
(5)

Multiplication and division with a scalar¶

Multiplying (or dividing) a matrix by a scalar means multiplying (or dividing) each entry by that scalar, e.g.:

cP=(cp1cp2cp3⋮cpN)c \mathbf{P} = \begin{pmatrix} c p_1 \\ c p_2 \\ c p_3 \\ \vdots \\ c p_N \end{pmatrix}cP=⎝⎛​cp1​cp2​cp3​⋮cpN​​⎠⎞​
(6)

where ccc is a scalar.

Multiplication with other matrices¶

Multiplication of one matrix by another matrix is more complicated.

Multiplication of a vector by a matrix¶

Suppose we want to calculate Q\mathbf{Q}Q, the product of the matrix M\mathbf{M}M and the vector P\mathbf{P}P,

Q=MP.\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{M} \mathbf{P}.Q=MP.
(7)

where M\mathbf{M}M is N×NN \times NN×N and P\mathbf{P}P is N×1N \times 1N×1.

The ith row of M\mathbf{M}M is

(mi,1mi,2mi,3…mi,N−1mi,N)\begin{pmatrix} m_{i,1} & m_{i,2} & m_{i,3} & \dots & m_{i,N-1} & m_{i,N} \end{pmatrix}(mi,1​​mi,2​​mi,3​​…​mi,N−1​​mi,N​​)
(8)

To obtain the ithi^{th}ith entry in Q\mathbf{Q}Q, the transpose of the ithi^{th}ith row of M\mathbf{M}M is set alongside of P\mathbf{P}P, multiplied term by term, and summed:

qi=mi,1p1+mi,2p2+mi,3p3+⋯+mi,N−1pN−1+mi,NpNq_i = m_{i,1} p_1 + m_{i,2} p_2 + m_{i,3} p_3 + \dots + m_{i,N-1} p_{N-1} + m_{i,N} p_Nqi​=mi,1​p1​+mi,2​p2​+mi,3​p3​+⋯+mi,N−1​pN−1​+mi,N​pN​
(9)

Repeating this for each row in M\mathbf{M}M results in a vector Q\mathbf{Q}Q that is the same size as P\mathbf{P}P.

Note that this is possible only if the number of columns in M\mathbf{M}M is equal to the number of rows in P\mathbf{P}P. Matrix multiplication is defined only if this condition is met.

For example, the multiplication PM\mathbf{P} \mathbf{M}PM is not defined, because each row in P\mathbf{P}P has only one column. That means the term-by-term multiplication operation cannot be executed.

Multiplication of a matrix by another matrix¶

We now consider multiplication of two matrices, each of which has more than one column:

Q=SR.\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{S} \mathbf{R}.Q=SR.
(10)

where S\mathbf{S}S is NS×KSN_S \times K_SNS​×KS​ and R\mathbf{R}R is NR×KRN_R \times K_RNR​×KR​ (that is, S\mathbf{S}S has NSN_SNS​ rows and KSK_SKS​ columns and R\mathbf{R}R has NRN_RNR​ rows and KRK_RKR​ columns).

The multiplication operation is the same as for MP\mathbf{M} \mathbf{P}MP above, repeating for each column in R\mathbf{R}R as if it were a vector, and for each row in S\mathbf{S}S. The elements of Q\mathbf{Q}Q are then:

qi,j=∑m=1KSai,mbm,jq_{i,j} = \sum_{m=1}^{K_S} a_{i,m} b_{m,j}qi,j​=m=1∑KS​​ai,m​bm,j​
(11)

for i=1,2,…,NSi = 1,2,\dots,N_Si=1,2,…,NS​ and j=1,2,…,KRj = 1,2,\dots,K_Rj=1,2,…,KR​.

Three very important things to note about matrix multiplication:

The matrix multiplication operation is defined only if KS=NRK_S=N_RKS​=NR​.

That is, the number of columns in S\mathbf{S}S must be the same as the number of rows in R\mathbf{R}R. Otherwise the two matrices cannot be multiplied.

Matrix multiplication is not cummutative:

SR≠RS.\mathbf{S} \mathbf{R} \ne \mathbf{R} \mathbf{S}.SR=RS.
(12)

It is frequently the case that SR\mathbf{S} \mathbf{R}SR is well-defined but RS\mathbf{R} \mathbf{S}RS is undefined.

The resulting matrix, Q\mathbf{Q}Q, has NSN_SNS​ rows and KRK_RKR​ columns.

In the following discussion, though, we will mainly be talking about square matrices and vectors.

The identity matrix¶

The identity matrix of size NNN is an N×NN \times NN×N matrix in which all diagonal terms are 1 and all other terms are 0:

I=(100…0010…0001…0⋮⋮⋮⋮000…1)\mathbf{I} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 &\dots & 1 \end{pmatrix}I=⎝⎛​100⋮0​010⋮0​001⋮0​…………​000⋮1​⎠⎞​
(13)

Multiplication of a matrix by III always gives back the same matrix,

IM=M.\mathbf{I} \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{M} .IM=M.
(14)

Inverse matrices¶

There is no matrix divison operation.

However, many matrices (though not all) have an inverse, M−1\mathbf{M}^{-1}M−1, defined so that

M−1M=I.\mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{I}.M−1M=I.
(15)

Finding the inverse of a matrix is often equivalent to solving a the system of equations, and can be a lot of computational work. There are both analytical and numerical methods of finding whether a matrix has an inverse and if so calculating what it is. Those are covered in Linear Algebra courses. Here, it is important to know that numerical libraries like Numpy and computer algebra programs like Maxima, Sage, Matlab, Maple, and Mathematica all have extensive matrix manipulation functions.

Matrix models as approximations for structured populations¶

In modeling structured or interacting populations, biologists frequently encounter matrix population models of the form

Pτ+1=MPτ\mathbf{P}^{\tau+1} = \mathbf{M} \mathbf{P}^{\tau}Pτ+1=MPτ
(16)

where

M=(m1,1m1,2m1,3…m1,Nm2,100…00m3,20…0⋮⋮⋮0…mN,N−10000…mN,N−10).\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix} m_{1,1} & m_{1,2}& m_{1,3} & \dots & m_{1,N} \\ m_{2,1} & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & m_{3,2} & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 0 & \dots & m_{N,N-1} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & m_{N,N-1} & 0 \end{pmatrix} .M=⎝⎛​m1,1​m2,1​0⋮00​m1,2​0m3,2​⋮…0​m1,3​00mN,N−1​…​………⋮0mN,N−1​​m1,N​0000​⎠⎞​.
(17)

In Equation (17), Pτ+1\mathbf{P}^{\tau+1}Pτ+1 is the predicted population in the next time unit[1], given a present population of Pτ\mathbf{P}^{\tau}Pτ.

That is, the model is discretized in two ways:

  • In time, with τ\tauτ representing successive time intervals, such as a day, a month, a year, etc.

  • In population structure, with the rows and columns representing an organismal classification, such as 1-years olds, adults, individuals within a given weight class, etc.

If the entries in Equation (17) are constant in time, then the matrix is called a Leslie Matrix and has some direct biological interpretations.

These can be made more clear by relabeling Equation (17) as

M=(b1b2b3…bN(1−μ1)00…00(1−μ2)0…0⋮⋮⋮0…(1−μN−2)0000…(1−μN−1)0),\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix} b_1 & b_2 & b_3 & \dots & b_N \\ (1 - \mu_1) & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & (1 - \mu_2) & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 0 & \dots & (1 - \mu_{N-2}) & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & (1 - \mu_{N-1}) & 0 \end{pmatrix} ,M=⎝⎛​b1​(1−μ1​)0⋮00​b2​0(1−μ2​)⋮…0​b3​00(1−μN−2​)…​………⋮0(1−μN−1​)​bN​0000​⎠⎞​,
(18)

In Equation (18), the terms now have the following interpretations:

  • bi,i=1,2,…,Nb_i, i = 1,2, \dots,Nbi​,i=1,2,…,N now represent the fecundity of individuals of age class iii (how many age 1 individuals are “born” into the 1st1^{st}1st classe. at time τ+1\tau+1τ+1 for each individual of age iii at time τ\tauτ)

  • μi\mu_iμi​ is the mortality rate of individuals in the transition between age iii and age i+1i+1i+1. (1−μi)(1 - \mu_i)(1−μi​) is the corresponding survivorship. That is, the fraction (1−μi)(1 - \mu_i)(1−μi​) of individuals in the ithi^{th}ith class survive from time interval τ\tauτ to the next time interval, τ+1\tau+1τ+1.

In (18), it is assumed that individuals cannot live longer than NNN time units. This is shown by the fact that mN,N=0m_{N,N}=0mN,N​=0, i.e., the survivorship of the NthN^{th}Nth class is 0.

We could imagine alternative scenarios, for example populations that are structured by stage instead of age, where NNN represents a final stage (e.g. adulthood) in which individuals can survive for more than one time unit. In that case, mN,Nm_{N,N}mN,N​ would take on some positive value.

Matrix models such as (18) are commonly used by ecologists to predict the age distribution of a population and whether that population is increasing or decreasing. As you can imagine, there are many circumstances in which (18) is not an adequate model, because there are strong temporal variations or density-dependent effects. However, when this type of model is appropriate to describe a population of interest, even very approximately, it is often worth doing because this model can be thoroughly understood.

Stable Age Distributions and the Eigenvalue Problem¶

We begin by considering whether the model in (18) predicts a stable age distribution.

A stable age distribution is a proportion of individuals in each age class that can be maintained over time. That is, if the population has the stable age distribution at time τ\tauτ it will have the same age distribution at time τ+1\tau+1τ+1:

Pt=cV,  Pt+1=λcV\mathbf{P}^{t} = c \mathbf{V},~~\mathbf{P}^{t+1} = \lambda c \mathbf{V}Pt=cV,  Pt+1=λcV
(19)

In this equation, λ\lambdaλ is the relative size of the population at τ+1\tau+1τ+1 relative to τ\tauτ.

Note that a stable age distribution does not imply that the total population is constant – it may be increasing or decreasing – but only that the proportion of individuals within each age class remains constant across time.

It turns out that by looking for a stable age distribution, we will discover a way to predict the long-term trajectory of any age distribution, stable or not.

By substituting (19) into (16), we can see that if V\mathbf{V}V is a stable age distribution, then

MV=λV\mathbf{M} \mathbf{V} = \lambda \mathbf{V}MV=λV
(20)

This equation is in the same form as a very important class of problems in Linear Algebra, known as eigenvalue problems.

One key result about eigenvalue problems is that, for an N×NN \times NN×N matrix, there are NNN possible solutions:

MVn=λnVn , n=1,2,…,N.\mathbf{M} \mathbf{V}_n = \lambda_n \mathbf{V}_n~,~n = 1,2,\dots, N.MVn​=λn​Vn​ , n=1,2,…,N.
(21)

This is an extraordinary result tt means that

If the model has 20 age classes, there are 20 different age distributions that are stable.

That is, if the population starts with any one of these distributions, the age distribution will remain fixed (even if the number of individuals in that population changes).

The stable age distributions have another remarkable property, which is that they are orthogonal. This means that multiplying two stable age distributions gives one of two results:

Vi′Vj=0 , i≠j1 , i=j\mathbf{V}_i' \mathbf{V}_j = \begin{matrix} 0~,~ i \ne j \\ 1~,~i = j \end{matrix}Vi′​Vj​=0 , i=j1 , i=j​
(22)

One final remarkable property that the stable age distributions have is that they are complete, which means that any population can be written as a sum of stable age classes,

Pτ=∑n=1NcnVn=c1V1+c2V2+c3V3+⋯+cNVN\begin{align*} \mathbf{P}^{\tau} & = & \sum_{n = 1}^N c_n \mathbf{V}_n & = & c_1 \mathbf{V}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{V}_2 + c_3 \mathbf{V}_3 + \dots + c_N \mathbf{V}_N \end{align*}Pτ​=​n=1∑N​cn​Vn​​=​c1​V1​+c2​V2​+c3​V3​+⋯+cN​VN​​
(23)

Because of the orthogonality property, it is very simple to calculate the constants cic_ici​:

Vi′Pτ=∑n=1NcnVi′Vn=c1Vi′V1+c2Vi′V2+c3Vi′V3+⋯+cNVi′VN\begin{align*} \mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{P}^{\tau} = \sum_{n = 1}^N c_n \mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{V}_n & = & c_1 \mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{V}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{V}_2 + c_3 \mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{V}_3 + \dots + c_N \mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{V}_N \end{align*}Vi′​Pτ=n=1∑N​cn​Vi′​Vn​​=​c1​Vi′​V1​+c2​Vi′​V2​+c3​Vi′​V3​+⋯+cN​Vi′​VN​​
(24)

which simplifies to

Vi′Pτ=ci\mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{P}^{\tau} = c_iVi′​Pτ=ci​
(25)

This is very convenient, because we then can write down the whole population’s age distribution at τ+1\tau+1τ+1 as

Pτ+1=∑n=1NcnλnVn\mathbf{P}^{\tau+1} = \sum_{n = 1}^N c_n \lambda_n \mathbf{V}_nPτ+1=n=1∑N​cn​λn​Vn​
(26)

In fact, we can repeat this as many times as we want, so the general case of the population at τ+k\tau+kτ+k is

Pτ+k=∑n=1NcnλnkVn\mathbf{P}^{\tau+k} = \sum_{n = 1}^N c_n \lambda_n^k \mathbf{V}_nPτ+k=n=1∑N​cn​λnk​Vn​
(27)

One final result:

The λi\lambda_iλi​’s represent the intrinsic rate of growth of populations with the corresponding stable age distributions (Vi\mathbf{V_i}Vi​'s).

Typically, one of these growth rates will be the largest. Most computer programs to calculate λ\lambdaλ’s give you the results in descending order, so we will assume that λ1\lambda_1λ1​ is the largest.

If that is the case, then in the long run the population will have the age distribution

Pτ+k≈c1λ1kV1 ,k≫1 .\mathbf{P}^{\tau+k} \approx c_1 \lambda_1^k \mathbf{V}_1~, k \gg 1~.Pτ+k≈c1​λ1k​V1​ ,k≫1 .
(28)

This is true for almost any starting conditions (if you try you can find pathological cases for which c1=0c_1 = 0c1​=0, but those usually aren’t biologically important).

The reason is that the higher exponential growth rate corresponding to λ1\lambda_1λ1​ and V1\mathbf{V}_1V1​ eventually swamps out all the other components. How long “eventually” is depends on the values in the matrix M\mathbf{M}M. If M\mathbf{M}M is such that λ2\lambda_2λ2​ is only a little smaller than λ1\lambda_1λ1​, then it may take many time steps for the population to approach V1\mathbf{V}_1V1​. If the difference is large, it takes just a few time steps.

Sensitivity and elasticity¶

A common application of matrix models is in formulation of conservation and management strategies for potentially at-risk populations. This is known in some circles as Population Viability Analysis or PVA.

PVAs can take on various forms, but a common approach is to formulate a matrix model that projects whether a population is likely to increase or decrease, and what the effects of possible demographic changes might be.

For example, in the case of a harvested fish population, management strategies could include size/age restrictions, marine reserves, restrictions on fishing seasons, etc. It is often far from clear what the impact of each of these strategies would be. Still, it is necessary to estimate those impacts to formulate rational conservation strategies, particularly because there are typically severe costs and constraints in implementing them.

One approach to quantifying the impacts of a strategy is to estimate how it would change the coefficients in the matrix MMM, and then calculate the consequences for the population growth rate λ1\lambda_1λ1​.

A common way to quantify this is the sensitivity,

Si,j=∂λ1∂mi,jS_{i,j} = \frac{\partial \lambda_1}{\partial m_{i,j}}Si,j​=∂mi,j​∂λ1​​
(29)

If Si,jS_{i,j}Si,j​ is large in magnitude, then a small change in the coefficient mi,jm_{i,j}mi,j​ has a relatively large impact on the growth rate. Conversely, if Si,jS_{i,j}Si,j​ is small in magnitude a small change in the coefficient mi,jm_{i,j}mi,j​ has relatively little effect. Of two equally costly management options, the matrix model approach might suggest that the option with the greatest (positive) sensitivity would be the more efficient strategy.

If the coefficients themselves of very different magnitudes, this estimate may be misleading. An attempt to correct for distortions of this kind is the elasticity,

Ei,j=mi,jλ1∂λ1∂mi,jE_{i,j} = \frac{m_{i,j}}{\lambda_1} \frac{\partial \lambda_1}{\partial m_{i,j}}Ei,j​=λ1​mi,j​​∂mi,j​∂λ1​​
(30)

in which the change population growth rate is expressed as a relative rather than absolute effect.
