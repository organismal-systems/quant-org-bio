📖️️ Size-dependent changes in excess density

School of Oceanography, University of Washington

The amount by which the density of a material exceeds the density of the fluid in which it is immersed is called excess density, ρexcess\rho_{excess}ρexcess​. The net body force on an object is equal to gravity times the volume of the object, V=L3V = L^3V=L3, multiplied by its excess density.

Because the excess density is an expression of the difference between gravity and buoyancy forces, Equation (1) is a restatement of Equation (3) from the previous page.

McDonald & Grunbaum (2010) found that larval swimming speed did not decrease as suggested by the allometries of ciliary propulsion and larval volume because the excess density decreased with larval size. These changes were not subtle: the largest larvae measured had roughly 110\frac{1}{10}101​ the excess density of the smallest. McDonald & Grunbaum (2010) hypothesized that this extreme decrease in excess density with increases in larval size were a specific adaptation to enable meeting a “standard” requirement for swimming performance.

McDonald & Grunbaum (2010) suggested that excess density in early stage marine invertebrate larvae changes by just the amount needed to compensate for the scaling of gravity and ciliary forces. The resulting shift in the ratio of body to surface forces results in upwards swimming that barely – but consistently – meets the “standard” performance requirement, varying little across a wide range of early-stage larval sizes.

Morphological aspects of excess density¶

How can excess density vary to this extent, if the material properties of larval tissue stay approximately constant (or, at least, vary by nowhere near a factor of 10)? The answer lies at least partly in the internal morphologies of early stage larvae. As illustrated in Figure 1, early stage marine invertebrate larvae have internal structures such as blastocoels. These structures enclose non-tissue materials, which may include fluid of unknown composition, lipids, calcium carbonate or other substances.

Those materials, which differ in excess density from ordinary embryonic tissue, have several distinct effects on swimming biomechanics:

  • They change the overall average excess density of a larva, which is the excess density that determines the overall body forces on an immersed object. For example, if a larva contains a given amount of tissue, its average excess mass is reduced if that tissue encloses a volume of fluid or lipid of relatively low density.

  • Also, for isometric size increases, that decrease in excess mass is accompanied by an increase in external surface area to accommodate the increased internal volume. If this additional surface area is ciliated, then the total ciliary propulsive force increases in rough proportion.

In other words, the hypothesis is that:

A “standard” level of swimming performance can maintained with increases in the size of early-stage embryos if, while their external shapes enlarge isometrically, their low-density internal structures change disproportionately faster.
