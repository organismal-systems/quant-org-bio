🏞️ Size and swimming in early-stage marine invertebrate larvae

School of Oceanography, University of Washington

Among the most basic tasks that must be accomplished by organisms is reproduction – the successful renewal of the population and passing of genes to the next generations. In a typical marine invertebrate life history, reproduction occurs episodically: Gametes (or their precursors) are accumulated over a prolonged period of months or even years. This is followed, in most cases, by a spawning event in which gametes are released into the water for fertilization.[1]

Fertilization is followed by development through a species-specific sequence of embroyonic and larval stages (Figure 1), usually during a planktonic phase.[2] The planktonic phase ends when the larvae achieve competence, meaning they have arrived at a state of physiological development in which the appropriate environmental cues can stimulate them to metamorphose. These cues indicate a suitable habitat for a juvenile to survive and mature into a reproductive adult.

In addition to development, many other important processes occur during the embryonic and larval planktonic period. Embryos and larvae are dispersed by transport in currents, often achieving competence far from their maternal habitat. Embryos and larvae are subject to predation, pathogens and other mortality mechanisms. In some cases, larvae feed and acquire resources for further development and juvenile survival.

Biotic and abiotic constraints on egg size and early development in marine invertebrate larvae¶

Like all organisms, marine invertebrates are limited by their metabolic resources. Among the most important ways in which resource limitations are expressed are constraints on reproduction. An important example of resource limitation is the trade-off that reproducing females confront between making fewer larger eggs vs. making more numerous but smaller eggs.

Larger eggs typically produce larger larvae that develop more quickly, and may suffer lower mortality due to predation or starvation. Conversely, smaller eggs typically require longer development times (or produce smaller juveniles) and hence may suffer higher mortality – but there are more of them to start with.

Is producing fewer large eggs that are more likely to survive, or more numerous small eggs despite their being more susceptible to mortality, a more successful strategy?

This is usually a complicated and difficult question to answer. For one thing, it is difficult to measure mortality in planktonic populations. Furthermore, changes in egg size can also have diverse, often subtle consequences. For example, longer development times in the plankton typically mean longer dispersal times and distances. Changes in egg size may therefore be geographically linked to changes in habitats and range. Changes in egg size may also affect metabolic rates, fertilization success, exposure to environmental fluctuations and many other aspects of marine invertebrate larval ecology.

It has also been hypothesized that egg size is directly related to constraints on swimming in early-stage larvae.

Why swim?¶

The fate of embryos and larvae during the planktonic period can be strongly influenced by their ability, or lack of ability, to swim. Multiple lines of evidence suggest that there is strong selection for swimming ability among diverse marine invertebrate embryos and larvae. Some of this evidence centers on how widespread and prominent morphological features for swimming are, and how quickly they are expressed. For example, Staver & Strathmann (2002)'s study of larval development showed that, across a wide diversity of taxa, development of cell lines required for swimming undergo exceptionally rapid differentiation. This accelerated development of swimming-specific features typically enable swimming in less than 24 hours, in some cases less than 10 hours, during a planktonic development period that may last several weeks. Prioritization of swimming in diverse taxa among the many competing needs in early development suggests that its role is fundamentally important to reproductive success.

Additional evidence comes from the strong variations across depth of many environmental characteristics critical to larval survival and growth in many marine environments. Most water columns show strong variations in temperature, salinity, food availabilty, predator risk, acidity, oxygen concentration, UV exposure and many other water quality parameters. For example, the benthos – on or adjacent to the substrate – is often considered an especially high predation risk environment (Allen & McAlister, 2007). Early swimming enables larvae to quickly escape this high-risk zone. In addition, because currents often vary with depth, water column position often has strong impacts on larval dispersal. More generally, individual larvae and embryos frequently encounter localized “patches” of food, predators and favorable or unfavorable microenvironments. In all of these situations, even a limited swimming ability can change the environmental conditions experienced by embryos and larvae in ways that suggest strong selective benefits in survival and development.
