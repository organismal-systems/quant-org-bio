📖️️ Allometries in early larval swimming

School of Oceanography, University of Washington

While there is good evidence that swimming is important in early-stage marine invertebrate larvae, there is much less clarity on exactly why it is important, how swimming confers benfits, and what are the minimal requirements for swimming performance to achieve those benefits. A study by McDonald & Grunbaum (2010) asked whether there is evidence, in the swimming of extant early-stage larvae, of a minimum threshold or standard for upward swimming performance.

The hypothesized swimming performance standard is based on the idea that, across a diversity of taxa and their habitats, the benefits of swimming acrue only if it typically results in modulating environments, avoids predators or accomplishes other movement-related tasks to a meaningful extent within a meaningful time frame. To the extent that this depends on shared environments, predators, etc., the minimal swimming characteristics necessary to attain these benefits may also be similar across diverse taxa.

This idea is vague and difficult to assess in terms of swimming costs and benefits. However, because the morphological traits that enable swimming are likely to impinge on other selective pressures on larval form, optimizing swimming performance likely has costs. If so, it would suggest that morphological traits promoting swimming would be shaped to the point that meets a hypothetical standard requirement, but does not exceed it.

Consistent with this idea, McDonald & Grunbaum (2010) found that, among the early-stage larvae of 13 diverse taxa with volumes spanning almost two orders of magnitude, all but the largest few performed upwards swimming at rates around 300−400μms300-400 \frac{\mu m}{s}300−400sμm​.

Swimming allometries¶

McDonald & Grunbaum (2010)'s finding is interesting, because a nearly constant upswimming speed across wide variation in size does not follow inevitably from simple allometries of swimming by ciliated spheroids.

In particular, the expectation for ciliary propulsion is that the propulsive force, FciliaF_{cilia}Fcilia​, should increase roughly in proportion to the surface area of a ciliated spheroid,

Fcilia∝L2F_{cilia} \propto L^2Fcilia​∝L2
(1)

where LLL is the “characteristic” length scale of the larvae (e.g. the cube root of its volume). Reynolds number analysis shows that the fluid dynamical drag force also scales as L2L^2L2.

In constrast, the force of gravity, FgravityF_{gravity}Fgravity​, is proportional to mass. Assuming isometric size changes and constant material properties, the gravity force that must be overcome by ciliary propulsion increases in proportion to volume,

Fgravity∝L3F_{gravity} \propto L^3Fgravity​∝L3
(2)

The buoyancy force, FbuoyF_{buoy}Fbuoy​, has the same scaling, so that the net “body” force,

Fbody=Fgravity−FbuoyF_{body} = F_{gravity}-F_{buoy}Fbody​=Fgravity​−Fbuoy​
(3)

that must be overcome by ciliary propulsion increases in proportion to L3L^3L3.

Clearly, as the length scale LLL increases, the body forces (gravity and buoyancy) will exceed the surface force (ciliary propulsion), so that increasingly large larvae will at some point be unable to propel themselves upward. Even before this limit is reached, the allometries of surface and body forces suggest that swimming speed should vary strongly with larval size. However, McDonald & Grunbaum (2010)'s observations show that this expectation is not reflected in the actual swimming performance of early stage larvae.
