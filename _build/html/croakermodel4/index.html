Stable age distributions and population growth rates¶

Leslie matrices have some properties that are extremely useful for applications in population modeling. Summarizing from the overview of matrix methods used in population modeling:

  1. The population in a Leslie matrix model always approaches a stable age distribution, in which the proportion of the population in each age class remains constant.

  2. The size of the population in this stable age distribution always increases or decreases geometrically, by a constant factor each time increment (in this case, each year).

To understand how Diamond et al. (2000) utilized these facts, we need to define some notation:

  • P^\mathbf{\hat{P}}P^ is the stable age distribution approached by the croaker population.

    • If the population is proportional to P^\mathbf{\hat{P}}P^ in year ttt, it is also proportional to P^\mathbf{\hat{P}}P^ in year t+1t+1t+1.

    • From any starting age distribution, the croaker population will converge to having an age distribution increasingly similar to P^\mathbf{\hat{P}}P^ over each year-to-year transition.

    • P^\mathbf{\hat{P}}P^ is typically normalized, to have a unit population size or “vector length”.

  • λ\lambdaλ is the rate of geometric increase or decrease of the population, which is the discrete-time equivalent of Exponential growth.

    • If λ>1\lambda>1λ>1, the population has a positive intrinsic rate of growth (i.e., it is growing).

    • If λ<1\lambda<1λ<1, the population has a negative intrinsic rate of growth (i.e., it is declining).

    • If λ=1\lambda=1λ=1, the population has a zero intrinsic rate of growth (i.e., it is constant).

Putting these facts together, we can say that, in the long run (after initial transients have passed), the croaker age distribution in Diamond et al. (2000)'s model will always converge to P^\mathbf{\hat{P}}P^. Specifically,

  • After the initial transients, the population in year ttt is related to the population in year t+1t+1t+1 by

Elasticities¶

Because the intrinsic rate of growth, λ\lambdaλ, is such a useful summary of the long-term trajectory of a croaker population in their model, Diamond et al. (2000) investigated how changes in various natural and human-influenced factors affected this rate.

Specifically, they calculated a metric called the elasticity to quantify the sensitivity of λ\lambdaλ to various parameters. Again summarizing from the overview of matrix methods used in population modeling, the elasticity for a given parameter mmm is defined as

Em=mλ∂λ∂mE_m = \frac{m}{\lambda} \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial m}Em​=λm​∂m∂λ​
(6)

EmE_mEm​ is most commonly applied in cases where the variable parameter mmm is an element in the transition matrix, A\mathbf{A}A, but it can also be very usefully applied to variables that contribute to those elements.

In our case, Diamond et al. (2000) calculated elasticities for the mortality rates and durations of each larval stage, as well as for the adult demographic parameters. To these, we can add our speculative Egg Investment Factor, EIFEIFEIF.
