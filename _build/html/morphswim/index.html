🏞️ Morphological requirements for swimming

School of Oceanography, University of Washington

As seen in Figure 1, marine invertebrate larvae often have intricate and beautiful forms. Larval morphologies have functional consequences for many aspects of life history, including swimming, detecting and avoiding predators, feeding, finding suitable substrate, etc. It is usually unclear which selective forces underlie the observed morphologies, or which phylogenetic constraints limit possible alternative morphologies.

Substantial empirical and theoretical research has been done on the biomechanical implications for swimming of larval forms such as those in Figure 1. This research suggests that many of the intricate morphological features of these larvae have functional effects (some positive, some negative) on swimming hydrodynamics.

Among other things, these features enable larvae to fulfill the basic requirements for successful swimming:

  1. Propulsion to move through the water; and,

  2. Stability and orientation for directed motion, especially in vertical directions.[1]

The fascinating and important work on well-developed larval forms suggests a further, frequently overlooked question:

How do embryos and other early-stage larvae, accomplish similar swimming performance with almost no specialized swimming morphological features?

Figure 1 shows the blastula stage in early larval development of three echinoids[2].

As shown in these examples, newly-hatched embryos and other early-stage marine invertebrate larvae typically have simple roughly spheroidal external surfaces, some or all of which are covered with cilia that provide propulsion. The internal morphologies are also typically quite simple, with an internal volume containing (a usually unknown) material that is sometimes accompanied by other inclusions such as lipid globules or calcium carbonate particles.

Despite their simple morphologies, nearly all blastulae and other early-stage larvae have a significant capacity to swim. This means that, at least at some times and locations within some habitats, these simple morphologies successfully solve the problems of propulsion, orientation and stability.

Their minimal but diverse morphologies, combined with their success as swimmers, raise interesting questions in biomechanics:

What are the constraints on larval size and shape resulting from the need to swim?

What are the constraints on swimming imposed by the need to satisfy other requirements and limitations in larval development?

How do each of these interact with environmental variations, and with the different life histories adopted by different taxa?

The remainder of this Unit address these questions, with an exploration of some ways in which the requirements for successful swimming may constrain early larval development, and other aspects of marine invertebrate life history.

Footnotes¶

  1. Aquatic environments often vary over much shorter distances in the vertical direction than in horizontal directions. For example, light levels often decrease over a few meters depth, but remain essentially similar for 10s or 100s of kilometers horizontally. Therefore, swimming to change exposure to environmental conditions is often much more effective in the vertical than horizontal directions.
