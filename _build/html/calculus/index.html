💡️ Calculus

School of Oceanography, University of Washington

The basic tool of calculus is the derivative, which represents the rate of change or slope of a function at a particular point.

As you can see from a sketch (Figure 1), you can obtain this slope by drawing a line through two points on the curve -- the point at which the derivative is to be taken, and another nearby point -- and sliding the second point closer and closer to the first point (without actually reaching it). The derivative is defined in the same way, using mathematical notation instead of the sketch.

For example, to find the derivative at t0t_0t0​ of the function f(t)f(t)f(t), we would calculate the function’s value at f(t0)f(t_0)f(t0​) and f(t0+Δt)f(t_0 + \Delta t)f(t0​+Δt) (the first and second points, respectively). The slope of the line between them is the ``rise over run’',

f(t0+Δt)−f(t0)Δt\frac{f(t_0 + \Delta t) - f(t_0)}{\Delta t}Δtf(t0​+Δt)−f(t0​)​
(1)

Sliding the second point towards the first amounts to taking the limit Δt→0\Delta t \rightarrow 0Δt→0. This means having Δt\Delta tΔt get closer and closer to 0, without actually substituting Δt=0\Delta t = 0Δt=0 (because that would be dividing by zero and wouldn’t give a meaningful result).

Using the limit, the derivative ( = the slope) of fff at t0t_0t0​ is

dfdt≡lim⁡Δt→0f(t0+Δt)−f(t0)Δt  .\frac{d f}{d t} \equiv \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{f(t_0 + \Delta t) - f(t_0) }{ \Delta t } ~~.dtdf​≡Δt→0lim​Δtf(t0​+Δt)−f(t0​)​  .
(2)

The ≡\equiv≡ symbol implies a definition, rather than an equality. So, the statement above reads

We define dfdt\frac{d f }{ d t}dtdf​ as …\dots…

Additional notation¶

Sometimes derivatives are written as

ddtf\frac{d }{ d t} fdtd​f, f′f'f′, or f˙\dot{f}f˙​,

all of which are equivalent (depending on the context) to dfdt\frac{d f }{ d t}dtdf​.

A well-written narrative will make it explicitly clear how to interpret notation for derivatives and all other applications.

Useful facts¶

Higher derivatives¶

  • The derivative of the derivative is the second derivative, written

    d2fdt2.\frac{d^2 f }{ dt^2}.dt2d2f​.
    (3)

    This represents the rate of change of the rate of change, which is the curvature of the function f(t)f(t)f(t).

  • The nth derivative is written

    dnfdtn,\frac{d^n f }{ dt^n},dtndnf​,
    (4)

    and is the result of sequential differentiation n times.

An intuitive example is position as a function of time, x(t)x(t)x(t):

  • The first deriviative of position, dxdt\frac{d x}{dt}dtdx​, is velocity; this makes sense because velocity is the rate of change of position.

  • The second deriviative of position, d2xdt2\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2}dt2d2x​, is acceleration; this makes sense because acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, i.e., the rate of change of the rate of change of position.

The Chain Rule¶

The chain rule applies in a case like f(g(t))f(g(t))f(g(t)), i.e., when fff is a function of ggg and ggg is a function of ttt.

  • The derivative of f(g(t))f(g(t))f(g(t)) with respect to ttt is just the product of two simple derivatives,

    dfdt=dfdg.\frac{d f }{ d t} = \frac{d f}{d g}.dtdf​=dgdf​.
    (5)

    This situation arises surprisingly often in mathematical biology, so the Chain Rule is a handy thing to remember.

Sums and differences¶

The derivative of a sum (or difference) of two functions is the sum (or difference) of the derivatives,

d(f+g)dt=dfdt+dgdt\frac{d (f+g) }{ d t} = \frac{d f }{ d t} + \frac{d g }{ d t}dtd(f+g)​=dtdf​+dtdg​
(6)

d(f−g)dt=dfdt−dgdt\frac{d (f-g) }{ d t} = \frac{d f }{ d t} - \frac{d g }{ d t}dtd(f−g)​=dtdf​−dtdg​
(7)

Products¶

The derivative of a product of two functions is

d(fg)dt=gdfdt+fdgdt\frac{d (f g) }{ d t} = g \frac{d f }{ d t} + f \frac{d g }{ d t}dtd(fg)​=gdtdf​+fdtdg​

d(f/g)dt=d(fg−1)dt=g−1dfdt+fd(g−1)dt\frac{d (f / g) }{ d t} = \frac{d (f g^{-1}) }{ d t} = g^{-1} \frac{d f }{ d t} + f \frac{d (g^{-1}) }{ d t}dtd(f/g)​=dtd(fg−1)​=g−1dtdf​+fdtd(g−1)​

Some functions and their derivatives¶

Constants¶

If ccc is a constant,

dcdt=0.\frac{d c }{ d t} = 0.dtdc​=0.
(11)

Polynomials¶

The derivative of tnt^ntn where nnn is any number \underline{other than zero} is

dtndt=ntn−1 , n≠0\frac{d t^n }{ d t} = n t^{n-1}~,~n \ne 0dtdtn​=ntn−1 , n=0

Natural logarithms¶

The derivative of log⁡(f(t))\log(f(t))log(f(t)) or ln⁡(f(t))\ln(f(t))ln(f(t)) is

dlog⁡(f(t))dt=1f(t)  .\frac{d \log(f(t)) }{ d t} = \frac{1 }{ f(t)}~~.dtdlog(f(t))​=f(t)1​  .
(13)

Note that log⁡(f(t))\log(f(t))log(f(t)) or ln⁡(f(t))\ln(f(t))ln(f(t)) are both ways to write the ``natural log’', which is the log base eee.

Unlike what we’re used to in some other contexts, in mathematics it is always assumed that log is the natural log, unless it is explicitly stated otherwise.

Log base xxx is generally written logxlog_{x}logx​, as in log10(f(t))log_{10}(f(t))log10​(f(t)) for base 10, log2(f(t))log_{2}(f(t))log2​(f(t)) for base 2, etc.

Exponentials¶

The derivative of exp⁡(t)=et\exp(t) = e^{t}exp(t)=et is easy to remember:

Integrals¶

There are two basic ways to think about integrals. The intuitive way is that integrals represent areas under a curve. For example, the area, AAA, under the curve f(t)f(t)f(t) between t0t_0t0​ and t1t_1t1​ is

A=∫t0t1f(t)dtA = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} f(t) dtA=∫t0​t1​​f(t)dt
(16)

The more mathematical way is to think about integrals is as anti-derivatives, so that if

F(t)=∫f(t)dtF(t) = \int f(t) dtF(t)=∫f(t)dt
(17)

then

f(t)=dFdt  .f(t) = {d F }{ d t }~~.f(t)=dFdt  .
(18)

A key practical fact is that we can get expressions for the derivatives of many functions. However, there are many functions whose integrals we cannot express analytically.

For many of those, we can get a numerical solution on a computer, although there are cases where that is also difficult.

Note that the first of the above examples is a definite integral, meaning that it has a specific range over which the integration is to be applied (in this case, between t0t_0t0​ and t1t_1t1​).

The second example is an indefinite integral, meaning that the range is not specified.

A key distinction between definite and indefinite integrals is that (assuming other parameters are specified) the definite integral has a specific numerical value, while the indefinite integral is a function (of the lower and upper limits).

If you remember that the integral means the area under the curve, then this distinction makes sense and is easy to remember – the area under a curve does not have a specific value until you specify starting and ending values.

Taylor Series¶

In many applications it is useful to approximate the values of a function in the neighborhood of a point as polynomials using the derivatives at that point. This approximation is called a Taylor Series, and is written

f(t1)=f(t0)+f′(t0)(t1−t0)+f′′(t0)2!(t1−t0)2+f′′′(t0)3!(t1−t0)3+…f(t_1) = f(t_0) + f'(t_0) (t_1 - t_0) + \frac{f''(t_0) }{ 2!} (t_1 - t_0)^2 + \frac{f'''(t_0) }{ 3!} (t_1 - t_0)^3 + \dotsf(t1​)=f(t0​)+f′(t0​)(t1​−t0​)+2!f′′(t0​)​(t1​−t0​)2+3!f′′′(t0​)​(t1​−t0​)3+…
(19)

In Equation (19), the ′'′ implies a derivative with respect to ttt, and the !!! implies the factorial (e.g. 4!=1×2×3×44! = 1 \times 2 \times 3 \times 44!=1×2×3×4).

The …\dots… implies that this is an infinite series, but in most applications only the first few terms are used.
