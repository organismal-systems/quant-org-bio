📖️ Model formulation: Year Class demography

School of Oceanography, University of Washington

To construct a population model, we need formulas that project the population in year ttt, P(t)\mathbf{P}(t)P(t), forward to year t+1t+1t+1. To understand how Diamond et al. (2000) formulated their model, we will go carefully through the three things that happen to the croaker population between the end of year ttt and the end of year t+1t+1t+1:

  1. Individuals age by one year
    That is, individuals who are in Year Class iii in year ttt are, if they survive, moved to Year Class i+1i+1i+1 in Year t+1t+1t+1.

  2. Individuals die
    Diamond et al. (2000) assumed that the probability of an adult dying in a given year is a constant, μadult\mu_{adult}μadult​, for all age classes older than larvae and juveniles (i=3,4,…i=3, 4, \dotsi=3,4,…, 8).

    Another way to express this is as a survival probability, SadultS_{adult}Sadult​. Because every adult will either die or survive, the probabilities μadult\mu_{adult}μadult​ and SadultS_{adult}Sadult​ must add up to one, so that

    Sadult=1−μadult.S_{adult} = 1 - \mu_{adult}.Sadult​=1−μadult​.
    (1)

    Combining aging and mortality, we can now say that the croaker population in Year Class i+1i+1i+1 in year t+1t+1t+1 is equal to the croaker population in Year Class iii in year ttt times the survival rate during that year:

    pi+1(t+1)=Sadult pi(t),p_{i+1}(t+1) = S_{adult} ~ p_i(t),pi+1​(t+1)=Sadult​ pi​(t),
    (2)

    for all the adult Year Classes, i=3,4,…i = 3, 4, \dotsi=3,4,…, 8.

  3. Individuals reproduce
    In assessing reproduction of fish such as croakers, it’s usually assumed that the main limitation is the number of eggs. That is because sperm are usually available in much greater numbers, most of which never encounter an egg and hence play no part in reproduction.

    To assess the number of eggs, we must focus on the population of female croakers. In some fish species the ratio of males to females is nearly equal (like it is in humans) and we could assume half the adults are female. However, in other fish species the sex ratio is strongly biased towards one gender (because of their basic biology, selective harvesting, etc.). In this case, it’s clearest to model males and females separately, and since the number of males is almost always sufficient to fertilize all the females, we don’t even have to worry about the males. So, we now make the assumption that we are modeling only females, and adjust populations, fecundity etc. accordingly.

    Let’s assume that a female croaker of age iii produces FiF_iFi​ female eggs. Accounting for all Year Classes, the population of female eggs (p1p_1p1​) produced at the end of year ttt to develop during year t+1t+1t+1 is then

    p1(t+1)=∑i=18Fipi(t)p_1(t+1) = \sum_{i=1}^8 F_i p_i(t)p1​(t+1)=i=1∑8​Fi​pi​(t)
    (3)

    In words, this equations says that:

    The number of eggs in year t+1t+1t+1 equals the number of females in Year Class iii in year ttt multiplied by the number of eggs produced per female (FiF_iFi​), summed across all the Year Classes i=1,2,…,8i=1,2,\dots, 8i=1,2,…,8.

Further assumptions¶

Two additional assumptions are worth emphasizing at this point:

  1. The demographic parameters (SiS_iSi​, FiF_iFi​, etc.) in Diamond et al. (2000)'s model are constant across years; in reality, there would be inter-annual variations, which might be quite large and exert strong effects on population dynamics.

  2. Diamond et al. (2000)'s model does not include density-dependence, in which demographic rates are affected by the size of the population (for example, a very high population might be food-limited and therefore grow more slowly, die faster or have lower fecundity).

These assumptions are important context when interpreting the model results.
