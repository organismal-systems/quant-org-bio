# 🏞️ Size and swimming in early-stage marine invertebrate larvae
Among the most basic tasks that must be accomplished by organisms is reproduction &ndash; the successful renewal of the population and passing of genes to the next generations.
In a typical marine invertebrate [life history](wiki:Life_history_theory), reproduction occurs *episodically*: 
Gametes (or their precursors) are accumulated over a prolonged period of months or even years.
This is followed, in most cases, by a [spawning](wiki:Spawning) event in which gametes are released into the water for fertilization.[^spw]

Fertilization is followed by development through a species-specific sequence of embroyonic and larval stages ([](comp-larvae)), usually during a planktonic phase.[^plk]
The planktonic phase ends when the larvae achieve *competence*, meaning they have arrived at a state of physiological development that the appropriate environmental cues can stimulate them to metamorphose. 
These cues indicate a suitable habitat for a juvenile to survive and mature into a reproductive adult.

Many important processes occur during the embryonic and larval planktonic period.
Embryos and larvae are *dispersed* by transport in currents, often achieving competence far from their maternal habitat.
Embryos and larvae are subject to predation, pathogens and other mortality mechanisms.
In some cases, larvae feed and acquire resources for further development and juvenile survival.







## Biotic and abiotic constraints on egg size and early development in marine invertebrate larvae
Like all organisms, marine invertebrates are limited by their metabolic resources, and among the most important ways in which resource limitations are expressed are constraints on reproduction.
An important example of resource limitation is the trade-off that reproducing females confront between making more smaller eggs *vs*. making fewer larger eggs.
Larger eggs typically produce larger larvae that develop more quickly, and may suffer lower mortality due to predation or starvation.
Conversely, smaller eggs typically require longer development times (or produce smaller juveniles) and hence may suffer higher mortality &ndash; but there are more of them to start with.

> Is producing fewer eggs more likely to survive, or more eggs despite their being more susceptible to mortality, a more sucessful strategy?

This is usually a complicated and difficult question to answer.
For one thing, it is difficult to measure mortality in planktonic populations.
Furthermore, changes in egg size can also have diverse, often subtle consequences.
For example, longer development times in the plankton typically mean longer dispersal times and distances. 
Changes in egg size may therefore be geographically linked to changes in habitats and range.
Changes in egg size may also affect metabolic rates, fertilization success, exposure to environmental fluctuations and many other aspects of [marine invertebrate larval ecology](wiki:Marine_larval_ecology).


## Why swim?
The fate of embryos and larvae during the planktonic period can be strongly influenced by their ability, or lack of ability, to swim.
Multiple lines of evidence suggest that there is strong selection for swimming ability among marine invertebrate embryos and larvae.
Some of this evidence centers on how widespread and prominent morphological features for swimming are, and how quickly they are expressed. 
For example, [](https://doi.org/10.2307/1543458)'s study of larval development showed that, in many species, development of cell lines required for swimming undergo exceptionally rapid differentiation.
This accelerated development of swimming-specific features enable swimming in less than 24 hours, in some cases less than 10 hours, during a planktonic development period that may last several weeks.
Prioritization of swimming among the many competing needs in early development suggests that its role is fundamentally important to reproductive success.

Additional evidence comes from the strong variations across depth of many environmental characteristics critical to larval survival and growth in many  marine environments.
Most water columns show strong variations in temperature, salinity, food availabilty, predator risk, acidity, oxygen concentration, UV exposure and many other water quality parameters.
In addition, because currents often vary with depth, water column position often has [strong impacts on larval dispersal](../../spatial-dynamics/EstuaryLarva/EstLarvSwim.ipynb).
More generally, individual larvae and embryos likely encounter localized "patches" of food, predators and favorable or unfavorable microenvironments frequently.
In all of these situations, even a limited swimming ability can changes the environmental conditions experiences by embryos and larvae in ways that suggest strong selective benefits in survival and development.

## Morphological requirements for swimming
As shown in [](comp-larvae), marine invertebrate larvae often have intricate and beautiful forms.
Larval morphologies have consequences for many aspects of life history, including swimming, detecting and avoiding predators, feeding, finding suitable substrate, *etc*.
It is usually unclear which selective forces underlie the observed morphologies, or which [phylogenetic constraints](wiki:Phylogenetic_inertia) limit possible alternative morphologies. 

Substantial empirical and theoretical research has been done on the biomechanical implications for swimming of larval forms such s those in [](comp-larvae).
This research suggests that many features of these larvae seem to have functional effects (some positive, some negative) on swimming hydrodynamics.
Among other things, these features enable larvae to fulfill the basic requirements for successful swimming:
1. **Propulsion** to move through the water, and
2. **Stability and orientation** for directed motion, especially in vertical directions.[^vrt]

The fascinating and important work on well-developed larval forms suggests a further, and frequently overlooked, question:
> How do embryos and other early-stage larvae, accomplish similar swimming performance with almost no apparently specialized swimming morphological features?

[](#comp-blastulae) shows the [blastula](wiki:Blastulation) stage in early larval development of three [echinoids](wiki:Sea_urchin)[^ech]. 
As shown in these examples, newly-hatched embryos and other early-stage marine invertebrate larvae typically have simple roughly spheroidal external surfaces, some or all of which are typically covered with cilia that provide propulsion.
The internal morphologies are also typically quite simple, with an internal volume containing (a usually unknown) fluid that is sometimes accompanied by other inclusions such as lipid globules or calcium carbonate particles.

Despite their simple morphologies, nearly all blastulae and other early-stage larvae are capable swimmers. 
This means that, at least at some times and locations within some habitats, these simple morphologies successfully solve the problems of propulsion, orientation and stability.

Their minimal but diverse morphologies, combined with their success as swimmers, raise interesting questions in biomechanics:

> What are the constraints on larval size and shape resulting from the need to swim?

> What are the constraints on swimming imposed by the need to satisfy other requirements and limitations in larval development?

> How do each of these interact with environmental variations, and with the different life histories adopted by different taxa?

The remainder of this Unit address these questions, with an exploration of some ways in which the requirements for successful swimming may constrain early larval development, and other aspects of marine invertebrate life history.

[^spw]: One constraint on spawning is that individuals (in some cases, whole populations) precisely synchronize their release of gametes  so that eggs and sperm encounter each before they are dispersed by currents and turbulence. Many taxa synchronize using environmental cues such as phases of the moon or storms, as well as chemical cue from conspecifics.
[^plk]: In some cases, fertilization happens internally. In many such cases, larvae are also planktonic, but some taxa undergo development within egg capsules or are brooded inside their mother.
[^ech]: Echinoids are sea urchins, which include the sand dollars.
[^vrt]: Aquatic environments often vary over much shorter distances in the vertical direction than in horizontal directions. For example, light levels often decrease over a few meters depth, but remain essentially similar for 10s or 100s of kilometers horizontally. Therefore, swimming to change exposure to environmental conditions is often much more effective in the vertical than horizontal directions.
