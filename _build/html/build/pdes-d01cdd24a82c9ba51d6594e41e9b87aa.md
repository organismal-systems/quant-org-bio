Consider a population that has an age structure, and which changes over time.  That population might be described by a function $p(t,a)$, where $t$ is time, $a$ is age, and $p(t,a)$ is the \textit{population density} of individuals of age $a$ at time $t$.  You can visualize this as a 3-dimensional surface plot of population (Figure \ref{Fig2}), with time along one horizontal axis and age along the other, and population density along the vertical axis.

How does such a population change over time?  The density function $p(t,a)$ has two independent variables ($a$ and $t$).  [In contrast, recall that the ODEs we looked at had only one independent variable (usually time, $t$).] If a conserved quantity (in this case population) is a function of more than one independent variable, then derivatives which involve that function are called \textit{partial derivatives}. Equations that contain partial derivatives are called \textit{partial differential equations}, or PDEs.  

PDEs arise frequently in models of structured populations, physics (and therefore physical biology!), and a great many other real-world phenomena. PDEs are simply mathematical statements about the dynamics of systems with more than one independent variable (in this case, time, $t$, and age, $a$). Unfortunately, PDEs are generally more difficult to solve than ODEs.  Even so, many useful analytical and numerical results exist about them that can be used to gain biological insight.  

To use these, we need to define some concepts and terms.

\subsection{Partial derivatives}
In Section 1, we defined a function's derivative as the slope of the tangent to the function at a given point.  As you can see from Figure \ref{Fig2}, there is a complication in applying the idea of a derivative to a function of two or more independent variables: the tangent is not unique.  Depending on which direction we look when we take the tangent, the slope may take on different values.  However, if we specify the direction, then the slope is unique and we can use it to define a derivative.  This is the idea behind \textit{partial derivatives}.

Partial derivatives are written with the $\p$ symbol instead of $d$, as in ${\p p \o \p t}$ (which is the partial derivative with respect to $t$) and ${\p p \o \p a}$ (the partial derivative with respect to $a$).  In each case, the interpretation of a partial derivative is that all the independent variables except the named one are held constant.  For example, in the case of ${\p p \o \p t}$, the independent variable $a$ is held temporarily constant.  

Graphically, ${\p p \o \p t}$ corresponds to choosing a point within the 3-d surface plot of $p(t,a)$, looking in the positive $t$ direction, and measuring the slope. Similarly, ${\p p \o \p a}$ corresponds to choosing a point within the 3-d surface plot of $p(t,a)$, looking in the positive $a$ direction, and measuring the slope.

\subsection{Fluxes and conserved quantities}
One reason that PDEs are very useful is that they can be used to describe the \textbf{flux} of a conserved quantity along one or more of the independent variables.  A flux has both \textit{magnitude} and \textit{direction}.  For example, in the case of $p(t,a)$, individuals in the population are aging as time progresses.  This implies a flux of population in the positive $a$ direction.

PDE's describing conservation laws frequently appear in the form 
\begin{center}
time derivative = -- (gradient of the flux)
\end{center}
In this expression, the term ``gradient'' means the slope or rate of change in the direction being considered -- in other words, the partial derivative.

For example, the PDE describing the rate of change of the age-structured population, $p(t,a)$, can be written as
\begin{equation}
{\p p \o \p t}  = -{\p  \o \p a} (\mathbf{J}) \label{Jeqn}
\end{equation}
where the flux of individuals as they ``move past'' a given age, $a$, at a given time, $t$,  is $\mathbf{J}(t,a)$.  

In this case, the flux of individuals is
\begin{displaymath}
\textbf{J} = 1 * p
\end{displaymath}
(that is, individuals age by one second for each second of time that passes) and therefore the PDE for the age-structured population reads
\begin{equation}
{\p p \o \p t} = -  {\p  \o \p a} \left(p \right) ~. \label{eq:eqnAGE}
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{eq:eqnAGE}) describes a population that does nothing but age (no demographics, emigration/immigration, etc.).  The left hand side is the time derivative.  The right hand side has minus the gradient, $-{\p  \o \p a}$, operating on the flux, which is $1 * p$.  In this flux term, the ``1'' represents the rate at which individuals are aging and $p$ is the number of individuals at that particular $t$ and $a$.

The same type of equation, with a different flux term, might appear if we are modeling a \textit{size-structured} population.  Suppose we follow members of a population and observe that the rate at which individuals increase in mass depends on their size (e.g., large individuals add more weight per unit time, until they reach maturity and stop adding weight).  Let's call the size-dependence of the growth rate
$g(w)$,  where $w$ is weight.  Then the flux might be 
\begin{equation}
\textbf{J} = g(w) ~p 
\end{equation}
and population would then be described by 
\begin{equation}
{\p p \o \p t} = -  {\p  \o \p w} \left( g(w) ~p \right) ~. \label{eq:eqnSIZE}
\end{equation}
Here, $p(t,w)$ is the population density of individuals with weight $w$ at time $t$.  

Note that in these equations, I have included the independent variables explicitly in some cases but left them implicit in others. That is common practice in the literature because including all of them all of the time can lead to long equations that are difficult to read. A compromise is to do as I have done above, which is to include the independent variables the first time a dependent variable is used but not otherwise (unless to indicate particular values of the independent variables). That makes for clearer reading, but it means that you must keep track in your mind of which independent variables govern each dependent variable.

As before, Equation (\ref{eq:eqnSIZE}) has only one dynamic process (individual growth) that changes the weight distribution over time.  However, this equation introduces an important variation, which is that the growth rate $g(w)$ can be a function of $w$.  That is, while all organisms age at one second per second, the rate at which they put on weight can vary with their size
% (and many other factors)
.  

This is true for fluxes in general.  For example, if (\ref{eq:eqnAGE}) were a \textit{stage-structured} model instead of age-structured, the rate at which an individual's stage changes over time would probably not be 1 -- it could vary with both stage and possibly other factors such as time (e.g. seasons), competition, etc. The usefulness of formulating models in the form of Equation (\ref{Jeqn}) is that they remain true no matter how the flux term varies with other parameters.  This makes them a simple and powerful way to structure and solve models.

We could add other terms, to obtain a more complete model.  For example, if individuals have mortality rates that are also a function of weight, $\mu(w)$, the population might be described by
\begin{equation}
{\p p \o \p t} = -  {\p  \o \p w} \left( g(w) ~p \right)  - \mu(w) p~. \label{eq:eqnSIZE2}
\end{equation}

Still another example is a \textit{spatially-structured} population, $p(t,x,y)$, that is diffusing over time in a
2-dimensional habitat (the \textit{x-y} plane).  In that case, the population might be described by 
\begin{equation}
{\p p \o \p t} = - {\p  \o \p x} \left( - D {\p p \o \p x} \right) - {\p  \o
\p y} \left( - D {\p p \o \p y} \right) ~. \label{eq:eqnDIFF}
\end{equation}
In (\ref{eq:eqnDIFF}), the fluxes in the $x$ and $y$ directions are $- D {\p p
\o \p x}$ and $- D {\p p \o \p y}$, respectively, and the $- {\p  \o \p x}$
and $- {\p  \o \p y}$ in front of them represent minus the gradients of these fluxes.  In this case, $D$ is a parameter analogous to a diffusion coefficient. The fluxes represent the action of the organisms' random movements (acting analogously to molecular diffusion and heat conduction) to transport $p$ down gradient from denser areas to less dense areas, at a rate proportional to $D$ times the gradient of population density.  Note that in this case, the flux has a different form than (\ref{eq:eqnAGE}), because flux is proportional not to the the population density but its derivative.
