# 🛼 Morphologies that meet the challenges of early larval swimming
The [preceding discussion](./SizeSwim.md) outlined the hypothesis that, to survive through development and successfully metamorphose into juveniles, many or most early stage marine invertebrate larvae must satisfy functional requirements for swimming performance.
Specifically, two hypothetical "performance standards" were proposed:
- **Swimming speed**
  
  The upward swimming speed of early stage larvae in still water must meet or exceed a minimum speed, $V_{min}$.
  The observations and analysis of [](doi:10.1093/icb/icq090) suggested that the minimum speed performance requirement is in the vicinity of $300-400 \frac{\mu m}{s}$.

- **Stability**
  
  Passive stability must be sufficient to maintain vertical orientation enabling upwards swimming under prevailing conditions of turbulence and shear.

The benefits and costs of swimming are complex, poorly understood and probably variable across taxa, over time and within different environments.
This makes it difficult to critically assess the tradeoffs implied by the hypothesis.
However, it is possible to quantify the biomechanical consequences of alternative early-stage larval morphologies, and to explore what (if any) constraints hypothetical swimming standards would impose on variations in size and shape.

The model in this Unit presents an opportunity to ask, across a range of larval sizes:
> - For a given amount of larval biomass, how are morphological variations constrained by hypothetical swimming requirements?  
> - Do swimming performance requirements for swimming speed and stability impose similar constraints, or are they different or even contradictory?

The sets of larval morphologies that satisfy (or fail to satisfy) the hypothesized swimming performance standards can provide a basis for comparison with observed larval forms, to assess whether there is evidence of selection to meet them.

## Model characteristics
The [model](./ChimeraSwim.ipynb) adopts a number of simplifications to make computations tractable and facilitate exploration of morphological parameters:
- The model assumes [low Reynolds number flow](../ReSphere/RSscaling.md), in which inertial effects are negligible and fluid forces are determined by the Stokes equations.
- The model implements early-stage larvae with external shapes constructed as [chimeras](./ChimeraDesign.ipynb) of two semi-spheroids, joined at a common equator.
- The model implements internal structure in the form of an inclusion, constructed as a chimera of two semi-spheroids.
- The model assumes that the embryonic tissue fills the volume inside the external surface but external to the inclusion; the inclusion contains a different material with a different excess density.
- The model approximates the localized effects of turbulence and other water motion with constant levels of shear or rotation.
- The model is executed in three stages:
  - The relationships between "organism-based" morphological parameters (*e.g.*, the amount of tissue, aspect ratio, relative inclusion size, *etc*.) and geometrical parameters (*e.g.*, diameter, length, *etc*.) are codified in [](./ChimeraGeom.ipynb).
  - Body forces and fluid dynamical characteristics are calculated and saved in a "morphology" file, in [](./ChimeraDesign.ipynb)
  - Movement of the larva from an initial position and orientation is simulated within a specified ambient flow, in [](ChimeraSwim.ipynb)

## Testing hypothetical performance standards: Parameter variation
The range of early-stage larval morphologies that can be generated by the model is quite broad, and includes a large parameter space.
To make exploration of a hypothetical performance standard for upswimming speed feasible within the constraints of an Activity, we will consider one test of the hypothesis to comprise measurement of upswimming velocity, across systematic variation in larval tissue mass and inclusion size.
Within one test, other parameters that determine shape and material properties are to be held constant.

### Fixed parameters
[](#parsF) shows the parameters to be fixed within a series of observations of model results constituting one hypothesis test.
That is, you are to choose a value for each of these parameters for one test, returning later to change one or more of them for an additional test.
:::{table} Table of fixed parameters
:align: center
:label: parsF
| Parameter | Symbol | Relevant range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aspect ratio | $\alpha$ | 0.5-2 |
| Relative equator position | $\eta$ | 0.2-0.8 |
| Relative inclusion position | $\sigma$ | 0.6-0.9 |
| Tissue density | $\rho_{tissue}$ | 1070 $\frac{kg}{m^3}$ |
| Inclusion density | $\rho_{inclusion}$ | 900-2669 $\frac{kg}{m^3}$ |
:::
The aspect ratio, $\alpha$, is a shape parameter determining the height of the larva relative to its diameter.
The relative equator position, $\eta$, determines how high is the widest part of the larva.
The relative inclusion position, $\sigma$, is the height of the inclusion within the external surface.
The densities $\rho_{tissue}$ and $\rho_{inclusion}$ are material properties of the tissue and inclusion.
The extremes of the relevant range represent lipid and calcite; the most parsimonious assumption is that the inclusion contains seawater ($\rho_{seawater}=1030 \frac{kg}{m^3}$) or freshwater ($\rho_{freshwater}=1000 \frac{kg}{m^3}$).

### Focal variable parameters
One test of the hypothesis consists of a systematic survey through the parameters in [](#parsV).
:::{table} Table of systematically varied parameters
:align: center
:label: parsV
| Parameter | Symbol | Relevant range |
| --- | --- | --- |
|Tissue volume | $V_t$ | 1 |
| Average excess density | $\rho_{excess}$ | 10 |
:::

Tissue mass is a metric of larval "size" that relates directly to trade-offs in egg size.
Average excess density (combined with the fixed parameters) is effectively a specification of inclusion size
That is, the external surface of the larva is "inflated" by expanding it isometrically, as much as necessary to achieve the specified average excess density.
For example, a larva with an excess density $\rho_{excess}=1050 \frac{kg}{m^3}$ has density midway between $\rho_{tissue}$ and $\rho_{inclusion}$.
Therefore, the larva must comprise 50% tissue volume and 50% inclusion volume, so its total enclosed volume would be $2 V_t$.

### Additional assumptions
To reduce the parameter space, it is assumed that:
- The shape of the inclusion is the same as the shape of the external surface
- The ciliary speed (that is, the water velocity generated by cilia at the larval surface) is $V_{cilia} = 1000 \frac{\mu m}{s}$

## Rationale: Testing a hypothetical performance standard for stability
Early stage larvae must be capable of maintaining vertical orientation in the shear and turbulence characteristic of the habitats in which they undergo development.



biomechanical requirements 




hypotheses/questions:

> Larval morphologies must provide passive stability in excess of the torques imposed on them by turbulence and shear.

> Is producing fewer large eggs that are more likely to survive, or more small eggs despite their being more susceptible to mortality, a more sucessful strategy?


> How do embryos and other early-stage larvae, accomplish similar swimming performance with almost no apparently specialized swimming morphological features?


> What are the constraints on larval size and shape resulting from the need to swim?

> What are the constraints on swimming imposed by the need to satisfy other requirements and limitations in larval development?

> How do each of these interact with environmental variations, and with the different life histories adopted by different taxa?
