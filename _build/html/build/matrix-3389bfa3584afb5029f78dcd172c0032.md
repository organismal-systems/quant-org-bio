# 💡️ Vectors, Matrices and Linear Algebra
Models that involve several interacting populations or sub-populations can involve lots of terms, and manipulating the resulting equations can get pretty messy.
To make handling such *systems of equations* more convenient, mathematicians have invented *vector and matrix
notation*.
Vectors and matrices allow us to write these equations in a concise way, so we can see the forest for the trees.

## Basic Matrix Facts

A **matrix** is a rectangular array of numbers, such as 

$$
\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix}
m_{1,1} & m_{1,2} & \dots & m_{1,N} \\
 m_{2,1} & m_{2,2} & \dots & m_{2,N} \\
 m_{3,1} & m_{3,2} & \dots & m_{3,N} \\
 \vdots  & \vdots  & & \vdots \\
 m_{N,1} & m_{N,2} & \dots & m_{N,N}
\end{pmatrix}
$$ (eqnMAT)
The example in {eq}`eqnMAT` is a *square* $N \times N$ matrix, but in general there can be any nunber of rows and columns.
- The elements of a matrix are indicated by subscripts: in $m_{i,j}$, 
  - the first subscript, $i$, specifies the row
  - the second subscript, $j$, specifies the column

A **vector** is either a row or a column of numbers, i.e., a $N \times 1$ or $1 \times N$ matrix, e.g.,

$$
\mathbf{P} = \begin{pmatrix}
p_1 \\ p_2 \\ p_3 \\ \vdots \\ p_N
\end{pmatrix},
$$ (eqnVEC)

- Vectors and matrices are often denoted in a way that distinguishes them from **scalar** variables.
  Common choices include:
  - boldface, such as $\mathbf{P}$ or $\mathbf{M}$
  - overbars, such as $\bar{P}$ for a vector and $\bar{\bar{M}}$ for a matrix
  
  Different conventions are used by different authors; a well-written presentation will indicate what is meant by each type of notation.

- The **diagonal** of a matrix is all the entries for which the row number equals the column number.

- The [transpose](wiki:Transpose) of a matrix is obtained by flipping along the diagonal, so that rows become columns and columns become rows.
  There are several frequently used notations for a transpose, including $\mathbf{P}'$, $\mathbf{P}^T$ and $\mathbf{P}^t$.
  For example, the transpose of {eq}`eqnVEC` is
$$
\mathbf{P}^T = \begin{pmatrix}
p_1 & p_2 & p_3 & \dots & p_N
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

## Matrix math
### Addition and subtraction
Matrix addition and subtraction are defined only if the two matrices are the same size, in which case corresponding entries are added or subtracted.
```{math}
\begin{pmatrix}
	a_1 \\ a_2 \\ a_3 \\ \vdots \\ a_N
\end{pmatrix}
+\begin{pmatrix}
	b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \\ \vdots \\ b_N
\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
	a_1+b_1 \\ a_2+b_2 \\ a_3+b_3 \\ \vdots \\ a_N+b_N
\end{pmatrix}
```

Matrix addition and subtraction are distributive:
$$
\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A} \\
\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B} = -(\mathbf{B} - \mathbf{A})
$$

### Multiplication and division with a scalar
Multiplying (or dividing) a matrix by a [scalar](wiki:Scalar_(mathematics)) means multiplying (or dividing) each entry by that scalar, e.g.:
$$
c \mathbf{P} = \begin{pmatrix}
	c p_1 \\ c p_2 \\ c p_3 \\ \vdots \\ c p_N
\end{pmatrix}
$$
where $c$ is a scalar.

### Multiplication with other matrices
Multiplication of one matrix by another matrix is more complicated.

### Multiplication of a vector by a matrix
Suppose we want to calculate $\mathbf{Q}$, the product of the matrix $\mathbf{M}$ and the vector $\mathbf{P}$,
$$
\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{M} \mathbf{P}.
$$
where $\mathbf{M}$ is $N \times N$ and $\mathbf{P}$ is $N \times 1$.

The *i*th row of $\mathbf{M}$ is

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
m_{i,1} &  m_{i,2} & m_{i,3} & \dots & m_{i,N-1} & m_{i,N}
\end{pmatrix}
$$
> To obtain the $i^{th}$ entry in $\mathbf{Q}$, the transpose of the $i^{th}$ row of $\mathbf{M}$ is set alongside of $\mathbf{P}$, multiplied term by term, and summed:

$$
q_i = m_{i,1} p_1 + m_{i,2} p_2 + m_{i,3} p_3 + \dots + m_{i,N-1} p_{N-1} + m_{i,N} p_N 
$$

> Repeating this for each row in  $\mathbf{M}$ results in a vector $\mathbf{Q}$ that is the same size as $\mathbf{P}$.

**Note that this is possible only if the number of columns in $\mathbf{M}$ is equal to the number of rows in $\mathbf{P}$. Matrix multiplication is defined only if this condition is met.**

For example, the multiplication $\mathbf{P} \mathbf{M}$ is not defined, because each row in $\mathbf{P}$ has only one column.
That means the term-by-term multiplication operation cannot be executed.

### Multiplication of a matrix by another matrix
We now consider multiplication of two matrices, each of which has more than one column:
$$
\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{S} \mathbf{R}.
$$
where $\mathbf{S}$ is $N_S \times K_S$ and $\mathbf{R}$ is $N_R \times K_R$ (that is, $\mathbf{S}$ has $N_S$ rows and $K_S$ columns and $\mathbf{R}$ has $N_R$ rows and $K_R$ columns).

**This operation is defined only if $K_S=N_R$ &ndash; that is, the number of columns in $\mathbf{S}$ is the sme as the numnber of rows in $\mathbf{R}$.**

The multiplication operation is the same as for $\mathbf{M} \mathbf{P}$ above, repeating for each column in $\mathbf{R}$ as if it were a vector, and for each row in $\mathbf{S}$:
$$
q_{i,j} = s_{i,1} r_{1,j} + s_{i,2} r_{2,j} + s_{i,3} r_{3,j} + \dots + s_{i,N_S-1} r_{N_S-1,j} + s_{i,N_S} r_{N_S,j} 
$$


As a result, 

would be repeated for each column so that again $\mathbf{Q}$ would
have the same size as $\mathbf{P}$.
Also, if $\mathbf{M}$ were not an $N \times N$ matrix but had $K$ rows, then $\mathbf{Q}$ would be a vector of length $K$.  In the following
discussion, though, we will mainly be talking about square matrices and vectors.

Note also that multiplication by the identity matrix,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{I} = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\
 0 & 0 & 1 & \dots & 0 \\
 \vdots  & \vdots  & \vdots & & \vdots \\
 0 & 0 & 0 &\dots &  1
\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
always gives back the same matrix,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{I} \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{M} .
\end{equation}

It is important to realize that \underline{matrix multiplication is not cummutative}, 
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P} \mathbf{M} \ne \mathbf{M} \mathbf{P}.
\end{equation}
There is no matrix divison operation.  However, many matrices (though not all) have an inverse, $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$, defined so that 
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{I}.
\end{equation}
Finding the inverse of a matrix is often equivalent to solving a the system of equations, and can be a lot of computational work.  There are both analytical and numerical methods of finding whether a matrix has an inverse and if so calculating what it is.  Those are covered in Linear Algebra courses.

Sage, Matlab, Maple, and Mathematica all have extensive matrix manipulation functions, so the best recommendation I can give in the context of this course is to consult the help facilities in those programs.

## Matrix models as approximations for structured populations
In modeling structured or interacting populations, biologists frequently encounter models of the form
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}^{\tau+1} = \mathbf{M} \mathbf{P}^{\tau} \label{eq:eqnPOP}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix}
m_{1,1} & m_{1,2}& m_{1,3} & \dots & m_{1,N} \\
 m_{2,1} & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\
 0 & m_{3,2} & 0 & \dots & 0 \\
 \vdots  & \vdots  & & \vdots \\
 0 & \dots & m_{N,N-1} & 0 & 0 \\
 0 & 0 & \dots & m_{N,N-1} & 0
\end{pmatrix} . \label{eq:eqnLESLIE}
\end{equation} 
In (\ref{eq:eqnLESLIE}), $\mathbf{P}^{\tau+1}$ is the predicted population in the next time unit, given a present population of $\mathbf{P}^{\tau}$.

If the entries in (\ref{eq:eqnLESLIE}) are constant in time, then the matrix is called a *Leslie Matrix} and has some direct biological
interpretations.  Relabeling (\ref{eq:eqnLESLIE}) as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix}
b_1 & b_2 & b_3 & \dots & b_N \\
(1 - \mu_1) & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\
 0 & (1 - \mu_2) & 0 & \dots & 0 \\
 \vdots  & \vdots  & & \vdots \\
 0 & \dots & (1 - \mu_{N-2}) & 0 & 0 \\
 0 & 0 & \dots & (1 - \mu_{N-1}) & 0
\end{pmatrix} , \label{eq:eqnLESLIE2}
\end{equation} 
the terms $b_i, i = 1,2, \dots,N$ now represent the *fecundity} of individuals of age class $i$ (how many age 1 individuals appear at time $\tau+1$ for each individual of age $i$ at time $\tau$).  $\mu_i$ is the *mortality rate} of individuals in the transition between age $i$ and age $i+1$.  $(1 - \mu_i)$ is the corresponding *survivorship}.  In (\ref{eq:eqnLESLIE2}), it is assumed that individuals cannot live longer than $N$ time units.  We could imagine alternative scenarios, for example populations that are structured by *stage} instead of age, where $N$ represents a final stage (e.g. adulthood) in which individuals can survive for more than one time unit.  In that case, $m_{N,N}$ would take on some positive value.

Matrix models such as (\ref{eq:eqnLESLIE2}) are commonly used by ecologists to predict the age distribution of a population and whether that
population is increasing or decreasing.  As you can imagine, there are many circumstances in which (\ref{eq:eqnLESLIE2}) is not an adequate
model, because there are strong temporal variations or density-dependent effects.  However, when this type of model is able to describe a
population of interest, even very approximately, it is often worth doing because this model can be thoroughly understood.

### Stable Age Distributions and the Eigenvalue Problem
We begin by considering whether the model in (\ref{eq:eqnLESLIE2}) predicts a *stable age distribution}.  A stable age distribution is a proportion of individuals in each age class that can be maintained over time, so that if the population has the stable age distribution at time
$\tau$ it will have the same age distribution at time $\tau+1$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}^{t} = c \mathbf{V},~~\mathbf{P}^{t+1} =  \lambda c \mathbf{V} \label{eq:eqnSTAB}
\end{equation}
In this equation, $\lambda$ is the relative size of the population at $\tau+1$ relative to $\tau$.  Note that the stable age distribution does not imply that the total population is constant -- it may be increasing or decreasing -- but only that the fraction of individuals within each age class remains constant across time.
It turns out that by looking for a stable age distribution, we will discover a way to predict the long-term trajectory of any age distribution, stable or not. 

By substituting  (\ref{eq:eqnSTAB}) into (\ref{eq:eqnPOP}), we can see that if $\mathbf{V}$ is a stable age distribution, then
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}  \mathbf{V} =  \lambda \mathbf{V}
\end{equation}
This equation is in the same form as a very important class of problems in Linear Algebra, known as *eigenvalue problems}.
One key result about eigenvalue problems is that for an $N \times N$ matrix, there are $N$ possible solutions:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}  \mathbf{V}_n =  \lambda_n \mathbf{V}_n~,~n = 1,2,\dots, N~~.
\end{equation}
This is an extraordinary result -- it means that if you have a model with 20 age classes, there are 20 different age distributions that are
stable (i.e., if the population starts with any one of these distributions, the age distribution will remain fixed).

The stable age distributions have another remarkable property, which is that they are *orthogonal}.  This means that multiplying two
stable age distributions gives one of two results:
\begin{eqnarray}
\mathbf{V}_i' \mathbf{V}_j =   \begin{matrix}
0~,~ i \ne j \\ 1~,~i = j
\end{matrix} 
\end{eqnarray}

One final remarkable property that the stable age distributions have is that they are *complete}, which means that any population can be
written as a sum of stable age classes, 
%\begin{equation}
%\mathbf{P}^{\tau} = \sum_{n = 1}^N c_n \mathbf{V}_n = c_1 \mathbf{V}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{V}_2 + c_3 \mathbf{V}_3 + \dots
%\end{equation}
\begin{eqnarray}
\mathbf{P}^{\tau} & = &  \sum_{n = 1}^N c_n \mathbf{V}_n  \non \\
  & = & c_1 \mathbf{V}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{V}_2 + c_3 \mathbf{V}_3 + \dots + c_N \mathbf{V}_N
\end{eqnarray}
Because of the orthogonality property, it is very simple to calculate the constants $c_i$:
%\begin{equation}
%c_i = \mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{P}^{\tau} .
%\end{equation}
\begin{eqnarray}
\mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{P}^{\tau} & = &  \sum_{n = 1}^N c_n \mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{V}_n  \non \\
  & = & c_1 \mathbf{V}'_i  \mathbf{V}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{V}_2 + c_3 \mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{V}_3 + \dots + c_N \mathbf{V}'_i \mathbf{V}_N \non \\
 & = &  c_i
\end{eqnarray}


This is very convenient, because we then can write down the whole population's age distribution at $\tau+1$ as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}^{\tau+1} = \sum_{n = 1}^N c_n \lambda_n \mathbf{V}_n
\end{equation}
In fact, we can repeat this as many times as we want, so the general case of the population at $\tau+k$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}^{\tau+k} = \sum_{n = 1}^N c_n \lambda_n^k \mathbf{V}_n
\end{equation}

One final result: The $\lambda$'s represent the intrinsic rate of growth of populations with the corresponding stable age distributions
(\mathbf{V}'s).  Typically, one of these growth rates will be the largest.  Most computer programs to calculate $\lambda$'s give you the
results in descending order, so we will assume that $\lambda_1$ is the largest.

If that is the case, then in the long run the population will have the age distribution  
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}^{\tau+k} \approx c_1 \lambda_1^k \mathbf{V}_1~, k \gg 1~.
\end{equation}
This is true for almost any starting conditions (if you try you can find pathological cases for which $c_1 = 0$, but those usually aren't biologically important). The reason is that the higher exponential growth rate corresponding to $\lambda_1$ and $\mathbf{V}_1$ eventually swamps out all the other components.  How long ``eventually'' is depends on the values in the matrix $\mathbf{M}$.  If $\mathbf{M}$ is such that $\lambda_2$ is only a
little smaller than $\lambda_1$, then it may take many time steps for the population to approach $\mathbf{V}_1$.  If the difference is large, it takes just a few time steps.


\section{Sensitivity and elasticity}
A common application of matrix models is in formulation of conservation and management strategies for potentially at-risk populations. This is known in some circles as *Population Viability Analysis} or *PVA}. PVAs can take on various forms, but a common approach is to formulate a matrix model that projects whether a population is likely to increase or decrease, and what the effects of possible demographic changes might be. For example, in the case of a harvested fish population, management strategies could include size/age restrictions, marine reserves, restrictions on fishing seasons, etc. It is often far from clear what the impact of each of these strategies would be, but it is necessary to estimate those impacts to formulate defensible strategies, particularly because there are real costs and constraints in implementing them. 

One approach to quantifying the impacts of a strategy is to estimate how it would change the coefficients in the matrix $M$, and then calculate the consequences for the population growth rate $\lambda_1$.
One way to quantify this is the *sensitivity},
\begin{displaymath}
S_{i,j}  = \frac{\p \lambda_1}{\p m_{i,j}}
\end{displaymath}
If $S_{i,j}$ is large in magnitude, then a small change in the coefficient $m_{i,j}$ has a relatively large impact on the growth rate. Conversely, if $S_{i,j}$ is small in magnitude a small change in the coefficient $m_{i,j}$ has relatively little effect. Of two equally costly management options, the matrix model approach might suggest that the option with the greatest (positive) sensitivity would be the more efficient strategy.

If the coefficients themselves of very different magnitudes, this estimate may be misleading. An attempt to correct for distortions of this kind is the elasticity, 
\begin{displaymath}
E_{i,j}  = \frac{m_{i,j}}{\lambda_1   } \frac{\p \lambda_1}{\p m_{i,j}}
\end{displaymath}
in which the change population growth rate is expressed as a relative rather than absolute effect.
