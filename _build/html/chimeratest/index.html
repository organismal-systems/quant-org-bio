🛼 Testing hypothetical performance standards: Parameter variation

School of Oceanography, University of Washington

The range of early-stage larval morphologies that can be generated by the model is quite broad, and includes a large parameter space. However, to explore a hypothetical performance standard for upswimming speed feasible within the constraints of an Activity, this parameter space must be condensed and prioritized.

In this Activity, we accomplish this by first switching from the geometrical parameter set in 🧮 Design and save early larval morphologies to the organism-based parameter set in 🧮 Geometries for swimming embryo chimeras, and then restricting variation to a subset of organism-based parameters.

Organism-based vs. geometrical parameters¶

The motivation for defining two sets of parameters is that they define larval morphologies in ways that lend themselves to two different purposes:

  1. Organism-based parameters are intuitive to use in posing biological hypotheses, because they are expressed in terms of organismal constraints such as the tissue volume, and measurable functional traits such as excess density.

  2. Geometrical parameters are necessary for constructing elements of the biomechanical model, but are unintuitive for posing biological hypotheses because they confound variations in multiple organismal traits.

Specifying either of these parameter sets also specifies the other. Because the translations between them are not straightforward, they are implemented in the two referenced notebooks.

Organism-based parameters¶

Organism-based parameters are those that reflect an organismal characteristic that is directly subject to selection or other constraints, or conveys an intuitive biological meaning.

The following organism-based parameters are necessary and sufficient to parameterize the ChimeraSwim model:

  • The aspect ratio, α=Ls0Ds\alpha=\frac{L_{s_0}}{D_s}α=Ds​Ls0​​​, is a shape parameter determining the height of the larva relative to its diameter.

  • The relative equator position, η=Ls2Ls0\eta = \frac{L_{s_2}}{L_{s_0}}η=Ls0​​Ls2​​​, determines how high is the widest part of the larva.

  • The relative inclusion position, σ\sigmaσ, is the position of the upper tip of inclusion within the external surface, as a fraction of total length Ls0L_{s_0}Ls0​​.

  • The densities ρtissue\rho_{tissue}ρtissue​ and ρinclusion\rho_{inclusion}ρinclusion​ are material properties of the tissue and inclusion.

  • Tissue volume, VtV_tVt​, is a metric of larval size that relates directly to trade-offs in egg size and other physiological limitations. It is equal to the total volume of the larva, minus the volume of the inclusion.

  • The average excess density, ρexcess\rho_{excess}ρexcess​ is a relatively easily measured larval characteristic[1].

In this Activity, we will consider one test of the “performance metric” hypothesis to comprise measurement of upswimming velocity, across systematic variation in larval tissue mass and excess density. Within that one test, the other parameters that determine shape and material properties are to be held constant. This can be followed, of course, with additional tests using other shapes and material properties, to explore the generality of the results of the first test.

Fixed parameters¶

Table 1 shows the parameters to be fixed within a series of observations of model results constituting one hypothesis test. That is, in 🧮 Geometries for swimming embryo chimeras, you are to choose a value for each of these parameters for a complete series of simulations (varying “focal” parameters) during one test, returning later to change one or more of them for additional tests.

Constraints on the relative inclusion position¶

The relative inclusion position parameter, σ\sigmaσ, uses a simple formula to position the inclusion within the external surface.

This parameter has two important constraints:

  • It cannot exceed σ<1\sigma<1σ<1

  • It must be sufficiently close 1 that the inclusion lies entirely within the external surface of the larva (this threshold differs across choices of other parameters).

The suggested strategy in this Activity is to leave this parameter at the default, σ=0.99\sigma = 0.99σ=0.99, at least initially. With this choice, nearly all larval forms generated with the formula contain the inclusion entirely within the external surface. However, it is possible to explore other (potentially more realistic) values of σ\sigmaσ, as long as you check to make sure the above constraints are satisfied.

Focal variable parameters¶

In the Activity, one test of the hypothesis consists of a systematic survey through the parameters in Table 2.

In 🧮 Geometries for swimming embryo chimeras, the excess desity ρexcess\rho_{excess}ρexcess​ functions effectively as a specification of inclusion size. That is, the model “inflates” the external surface of the larva by expanding it isometrically (keeping tissue volume constant, and adding inclusion volume) as much as necessary to achieve the specified average excess density.

For example, suppose a larva has tissue density of ρtissue=1230kgm3\rho_{tissue} = 1230 \frac{kg}{m^3}ρtissue​=1230m3kg​ and an inclusion filled with seawater, ρinclusion=1030kgm3\rho_{inclusion} = 1030 \frac{kg}{m^3}ρinclusion​=1030m3kg​. If this larva has an average excess density ρexcess=100kgm3\rho_{excess}=100 \frac{kg}{m^3}ρexcess​=100m3kg​, then its actual average density is ρexcess+ρseawater=1130kgm3\rho_{excess}+\rho_{seawater}=1130 \frac{kg}{m^3}ρexcess​+ρseawater​=1130m3kg​. Because this larva has a density midway between ρtissue\rho_{tissue}ρtissue​ and ρinclusion\rho_{inclusion}ρinclusion​, it must comprise 50% tissue volume and 50% inclusion volume, so its total enclosed volume would be 2Vt2 V_t2Vt​.

In this example, a lower ρexcess\rho_{excess}ρexcess​ implies a larger fraction of internal volume is inclusion, so its volume is larger. Conversely, a higher ρexcess\rho_{excess}ρexcess​ implies a smaller fraction of internal volume is inclusion, so its volume is smaller.

Additional assumptions¶

To reduce the parameter space, it is assumed that:

  • The shape of the inclusion is the same as the shape of the external surface.

  • The ciliary speed (that is, the water velocity generated by cilia at the larval surface) is Vcilia=1000μmsV_{cilia} = 1000 \frac{\mu m}{s}Vcilia​=1000sμm​.[2]

  • Larvae are axisymmetric, meaning that the inclusion is centered on the same vertical axis as the external surface.

Each of these “implicit” choices excludes alternatives that would likely have different swimming performance.

The choices of focal, fixed and implicit assumptions are to some extent arbitrary. These choices essentially reflect selection of parameters hypothesized to have the largest fundamental effects on performance as focal parameters. If this hypothesis is correct, then the implications of variation in these characteristics are most efficiently explored after some of the fundamental consequences of variations in size and excess density have been elucidated.
